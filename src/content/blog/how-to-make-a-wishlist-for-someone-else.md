My dad has never made a wishlist in his life. Not once. Not for a birthday, not for Christmas, not for his retirement. Every year, my mother asks him what he wants, and every year he says the same thing: "I'm fine. I don't need anything. Save your money." And every year, the rest of us spend December guessing, which produces a predictable result. He gets a couple of books he's already read, a sweater in a color he'll never wear, and at least one gadget that ends up in the garage within a week.

Two years ago, I stopped asking. Not because I gave up, but because I realized I'd been asking the wrong person. My dad can't articulate what he wants — it's a genuine limitation, not stubbornness. But my mom knows. His brother knows. The guy he plays golf with every Saturday knows. And I know, because I've been watching him for forty years.

So I made the list for him. Not a wishlist he'd fill out himself, but a gift list I built on his behalf, drawing on everything the people closest to him had observed. And then I shared it with the family using a hidden-reservation system so he'd never see who claimed what, and so the surprises stayed intact.

It worked better than any gift we've given him in a decade. But the process of building it taught me a lot about what it takes to make a wishlist for someone else — and about why this is one of the most useful skills in the whole gift-giving universe.

## When You Should Make a List for Someone Else

Not every situation calls for this. But several do, and they come up more often than you'd think.

**When the person is unable or unwilling to make their own list.** Some people genuinely can't — kids, elderly relatives with cognitive decline, people with disabilities that make list-building difficult. Others simply won't, because they find it uncomfortable, or they don't think about their own wants, or they come from a generation where asking was considered rude.

**When the gift is for a child.** Kids under a certain age can't build a meaningful list. Parents build it for them. Even older kids often need help — they know what they want in the abstract but can't translate that into specifics. I wrote about the kid version of this in my [kids' birthday gift guide](/blog/kids-birthday-gift-surprise/).

**When you're organizing a group gift.** A group needs a shared list. Sometimes the recipient builds it, but often someone else has to. This is especially true for [weddings](/blog/wedding-gift-surprise/), [retirement gifts](/blog/retirement-gift-surprise/), and [milestone birthdays](/blog/milestone-birthday-gift-surprise/), where the coordination itself is half the gift.

**When the recipient is hard to shop for and doesn't know it.** Some people insist they don't want anything, but they light up when they receive something they actually love. Their "I don't need anything" is not the truth; it's a defense mechanism. Building a list for them can bypass the discomfort entirely.

**When you want a surprise.** This is the one people don't think about. If the recipient builds their own list, they still don't see the reservations — assuming you're using a hidden-reservation platform. But if you build the list for them, there's an extra layer of delight. They didn't even know the list existed. The gifts arrive as complete surprises, not just in terms of which items, but in terms of the entire gesture.

## The Reconnaissance Method, Applied to People You Know Well

When you're building a list for someone else, the first step is observation. You're looking for clues that the person themselves might not be able to articulate.

**Listen for complaints.** "My back has been killing me." "This coffee maker takes forever." "My phone battery dies by noon." These aren't just small talk. They're the raw material of a good gift list. A person grumbling about a stiff back would love a good heating pad or a massage gift certificate. A person who keeps mentioning their dying phone battery would love a portable charger.

**Notice what's worn out.** Old slippers. A stained bathrobe. A scratched watch. A cracked phone case. A kitchen knife that's lost its edge. These are items people live with because replacing them feels like a chore. Replacing them is a gift that lands quietly and permanently.

**Pay attention to what they've stopped doing.** Did they give up a hobby because of cost, time, or age? Did they stop painting, fishing, playing guitar, gardening? Restarting something someone loved is a powerful gift. It says, "I remember who you used to be, and I want that for you again."

**Catch the passing wishes.** "I've always wanted to try that." "We drove past it once and I always wondered." "I used to love doing that when I was younger." These are the quiet dreams people don't pursue because they feel indulgent. They're usually the best gifts of all.

**Watch what they linger over.** In a store. On a website. In a catalog. If they stop and look, there's something there. Your job is to notice without making it weird.

If you want a deeper dive into this method, I wrote about it at length in my [hard-to-shop-for gift ideas](/blog/hard-to-shop-for-gift-ideas/) post. The techniques are the same whether you're building a list for your dad or shopping for a colleague you barely know.

## Recruit Allies (This Is the Step People Skip)

You don't have to do this alone. In fact, you shouldn't. The people closest to the recipient have information you don't.

**Talk to their partner or spouse.** They see the person every day and know things you don't. What have they been complaining about? What's on their "someday" list? What's the one thing that would make their life easier?

**Talk to their kids or siblings.** Adult children often know their parents better than they realize. Siblings have decades of shared history and know what the person was into before life got busy.

**Talk to their closest friend.** This is often the richest source of information. A best friend knows the hobbies, the dreams, the frustrations, and the small quirks that make a gift feel personal.

**Talk to coworkers or a boss.** For retirement gifts or work milestones, colleagues know things family doesn't. What's the person been working toward? What have they mentioned wanting for their next chapter?

The key is to ask open questions rather than narrow ones. "What should I get them?" produces the same answers everyone else gets. "What have they mentioned wanting lately?" produces gold.

## Building the List: Structure Matters

Once you have your observations, the structure of the list determines how well it works. Here's the framework that's worked best for me.

**Aim for 12 to 20 items.** Enough that givers have real choice, but not so many that the list becomes overwhelming.

**Include a wide range of price points.** This is the single most important structural thing. Your list should span from under $20 to a few hundred. That means:

- Three to five items under $25 (small treats, practical items)
- Five to eight items in the $25 to $75 range (the bulk of the list)
- Two to three items in the $75 to $200 range
- One or two contribution items where multiple people can chip in

**Write a note for each item explaining why it matters.** "A cast iron skillet — he mentioned his old one is losing its seasoning" is a great list item. "A pan" is not. The note does double duty: it helps givers understand the thought behind each choice, and it shows the recipient that someone was paying attention.

**Mix practical and aspirational.** Practical items help givers who want to feel useful. Aspirational items spark joy and are often the ones claimed first.

**Include at least one sentimental item.** A photo book. A book of notes. A framed print from a meaningful place. These cost almost nothing and mean more than anything else on the list. I wrote about the mechanics of these in my [milestone birthday guide](/blog/milestone-birthday-gift-surprise/).

**Consider experiences over objects.** Especially for people who don't want more stuff. A class, a trip, a gift certificate to a restaurant, a contribution toward something they're saving for. My [experience gift guide](/blog/experience-gift-surprise/) has plenty of ideas if you need inspiration.

For a much larger pool of specific items, my post on [what to put on a wishlist](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-wishlist/) has over a hundred ideas organized by category.

## The Secrecy Layer: How to Build a List Without the Recipient Finding Out

Here's where it gets tricky. When you build a list for someone else, the recipient almost certainly knows something is being planned. They may even know a list exists. The challenge is to make sure they don't see the reservations.

This is exactly the problem the hidden-reservation model solves. You create the list on a platform like Giftly Lists. You share the link with the people who'll be contributing. They can see what's been claimed. The recipient can't see any of it.

For a recipient who's never seen the list at all — like my dad — the coordination is entirely behind the scenes. For a recipient who knows a list exists — like a bride or a birthday person who's been told "we're coordinating something" — the hidden-reservation model still protects the specific surprises.

There's also a nice middle ground. You can share the list with the recipient in a limited way. They can see the items, add to the list, remove things they've bought themselves. But they never see the reservations. This works especially well for couples, where both partners can curate the list but neither sees what's been claimed.

## A Personal Story: The List That Finally Worked for My Dad

Let me tell you how my dad's list actually came together, because the process was messier than it might sound.

I started by calling my mom. She was skeptical. "He doesn't want anything," she said. "You know how he is." I pushed back. "He doesn't want anything he has to think about," I said. "But if something showed up that was exactly what he needed, he'd love it." She paused. Then she said, "Well, he has been complaining about the recliner for two years."

That was my first clue. I called my dad's brother. He mentioned that my dad had been talking about a book about a specific historical event — something he'd been meaning to read for a decade. I called the golfing buddy. He said my dad had been complaining about his old golf bag — the strap was broken and he'd been making do. And I called my wife, who had been observing my dad at family gatherings for years. She remembered that he'd once mentioned wanting a good weather station for the backyard.

Four clues. That was enough.

I built a list with eight items. The recliner was the big one — a contribution item, so multiple family members could chip in without anyone feeling stretched. The book was a small item, around $25. The golf bag was a mid-range item. The weather station was around $100. I added a few more items based on smaller observations: a really good pair of reading glasses, a pair of warm gloves for his early morning walks, a coffee subscription from a local roaster he liked, and a framed photo of our family from a vacation he'd loved.

I shared the list with my siblings and my mom, using a hidden-reservation setup. My dad had no idea. He didn't even know I'd been making phone calls. He thought we'd forgotten all about his birthday because I hadn't asked him what he wanted, which had become an annual ritual.

The day of his birthday, he opened the smaller items first. The glasses, the gloves, the book. He lingered on the book for a while. Then the weather station. He put it down and looked at me. "I've been wanting one of these for years," he said. "How did you know?"

And then the recliner. My siblings and I had pooled our money, and my wife's parents had contributed too, and the whole thing had come together quietly. When he opened the card explaining it, he didn't say anything for a long time. Then he looked at my mom and said, "Did you know about this?" She nodded. "I helped," she said. He looked at me. "You called people, didn't you." I nodded. He laughed. Then his eyes got a little wet, and he said, "Nobody's ever done that before."

That's the thing about making a list for someone else. It's not just about the gifts. It's about the effort of noticing. It says, "I've been paying attention. I know you, even when you can't tell me what you want." That's a gift in its own right.

## When to Let the Recipient Help

Some people don't want a surprise. Some want to be involved, to feel some control over what's coming. When that's the case, you can adapt.

**Let them build the list themselves.** This is the simplest version. They create the list, you share it with the givers, they see only the items. This works for most people.

**Let them curate with you.** You build the initial list, share it with them, and they add, remove, and adjust. Then they don't see the reservations. This is a nice middle ground — they have input on what's on the list, but the specific gifts are still surprises.

**Let them know a list exists but not what's on it.** Some people are uncomfortable with the idea of a list about them floating around. Tell them, "We're coordinating something, there's a list, but you won't see it. That's the whole point." Most people will accept this and enjoy the mystery.

**If they insist on seeing everything, respect it.** Some people genuinely can't handle surprise. If that's the case, they don't need a wishlist for surprise-protection — they just need one for organization. Still use a list to coordinate. Just let them see the reservations. That's their choice.

## A Note for the Recipient: If Someone Has Built a List for You

If you're reading this and you've just discovered that someone — a partner, a sibling, a parent — has been building a list on your behalf, here's my advice.

Don't be embarrassed. They did it because they care. It's not that you failed to communicate; it's that they wanted to do the work of noticing. Let them.

Don't try to figure out what's on the list. If they've kept it hidden, that's because they want the surprise to land. Enjoy the mystery. Look forward to it.

Don't compare it to what you would have chosen for yourself. The list reflects what the people around you see in you, which is sometimes different from how you see yourself. That's the whole point. It's a small mirror.

And when the gifts arrive, be gracious. Even if something misses the mark. The effort was real, even if the execution wasn't perfect.

## The Bottom Line

Making a wishlist for someone else is one of the most underrated gifts you can give. It's not the same as buying the gift itself. It's a step earlier — the step of noticing, gathering, remembering, organizing. It takes time and attention, and it produces something that the recipient might never have been able to produce for themselves.

If the person is impossible to shop for because they won't tell you what they want, the solution isn't to keep asking. It's to stop asking and start observing. And to stop trying to hold all the information in your head and start putting it into a list.

My dad's list is still going. We update it every year. He's never seen it. He's never seen the reservations. And every year, he opens something that makes him pause, and say, "How did you know?" And every year, we know a little better.

That's the whole thing.
