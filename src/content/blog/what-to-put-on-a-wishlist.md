I once spent forty-five minutes staring at a blank wishlist. Forty-five minutes. My wife had asked me to put together a list of things I wanted for my birthday, and I sat there with my phone in my hand, cursor blinking, completely paralyzed. I could think of things I needed — new socks, a replacement phone charger, a car wash — but those felt too boring to ask for. I could think of things I wanted — a new guitar, a weekend away, a fancy espresso machine — but those felt too extravagant. So I wrote nothing, closed the app, and told her "I don't really need anything." She sighed. I sighed. Nobody won.

What I've learned since then is that most people aren't bad at making wishlists. They've just never been taught how. We're conditioned to think that asking for specific gifts is demanding, so we default to vague non-answers that leave our loved ones floundering. But a good wishlist isn't a demand. It's a map. It's a way of saying, "Here are the things that would genuinely make me happy, at a range of price points, so you can choose what works for you." That's not greedy. That's helpful.

So I want to do something practical today. I've compiled a list of over 75 wishlist ideas, organized by category and price point, along with some guidance on how to build a list that people actually enjoy shopping from. Whether you're making a list for yourself, helping a kid build one, or trying to inspire someone who claims they "don't want anything," this should give you plenty to work with.

## Why Most Wishlists Fail

Before we get to the ideas, let's talk about what makes a wishlist fail. I've seen dozens of wishlists in my years of coordinating gifts, and the bad ones tend to share a few traits.

**They're too vague.** "Something for the kitchen" doesn't help anyone. "A cast iron skillet, 10-inch, pre-seasoned" does. The more specific you are, the more confident your givers feel.

**They're all one price point.** If everything on your list costs $200, anyone on a tight budget feels excluded. A good list has items at $10, $50, $100, and beyond.

**They're all the same category.** A list that's entirely books, or entirely gadgets, or entirely clothes, doesn't give people much choice. Variety makes shopping more fun.

**They skip the sentimental.** The best gift is often the one that says "I was paying attention to who you are," not "I bought you the thing you asked for." Include a few items that have emotional weight — a photo book, a handwritten recipe collection, a framed print by an artist you love.

**They don't get updated.** A wishlist that hasn't changed in three years is a wishlist nobody checks. Refresh it regularly. Add new things. Remove things you've bought yourself.

Now, with that out of the way, here are the ideas.

## How to Organize Your Wishlist So People Actually Use It

Now that you have ideas, here's how to turn them into a list that works.

**Group by category and price point.** If your list is one long undifferentiated stream, it's overwhelming. Sort it into sections — practical, fun, experiences, sentimental — and note the price range for each. This makes it easy for givers to find something that fits their budget and their relationship to you.

**Write notes for each item.** "A cast iron skillet" is fine. "A 10-inch cast iron skillet — I've been wanting to try making cornbread in one" is better. The note gives context and makes the gift feel personal.

**Include a mix of "need" and "want."** Some people want to give you something practical. Others want to give you something fun. Give them both options.

**Update it regularly.** A wishlist that's a year old is a wishlist nobody checks. Add new things as you discover them. Remove things you've bought yourself. A living list is a list that gets used.

**Use a platform with hidden reservations.** This is the part that makes the whole system work. If you're using Giftly Lists, your givers can see what's been claimed, but you can't. That means you can share your list widely without spoiling your own surprises. I've written about [how to share a wishlist without feeling greedy](/blog/share-wishlist-without-greed/), and the short version is: hidden reservations change everything. You get the coordination benefits without the spoiler problem.

**Share it clearly and warmly.** When you send your list to someone, include a note that explains the hidden-reservation feature. Something like: "Here's my wishlist for my birthday. The cool part: you can see what's been claimed, but I can't see any of it — so I'll still be surprised. Use it if you'd like, no pressure at all."

## A Note on Making Lists for Other People

If you're making a wishlist for someone else — a spouse, a child, a parent who won't make their own — the process is slightly different. You'll need to do a little reconnaissance. Talk to the people who know them best. Pay attention to what they mention wanting. Look at what they already own and love. Then build a list that reflects their actual tastes, not your assumptions.

If you're making a list for a child, involve them. Ask them what they'd love. Help them categorize. The process of making a list is itself a fun activity, and it teaches them to think about what they want and why. Plus, they'll be more invested in the list if they helped make it.

## The Bottom Line

A wishlist isn't a demand. It's a gift to the people who love you. It says, "I want to make this easier for you. I want you to feel confident that your gift will land. I want to experience the joy of being surprised by something I actually wanted."

If you've been hesitant to make a list — because it feels greedy, or demanding, or awkward — I hope this post has changed your mind. The people in your life want to give you good gifts. They just need a little help. A well-made wishlist is that help. And with the right platform, it can preserve the surprise while making the whole process smoother for everyone.

So start your list. Add a few things. Share it with someone. And then let yourself be surprised.
