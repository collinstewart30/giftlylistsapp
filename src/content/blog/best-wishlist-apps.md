For years, my family's gift coordination lived in a group chat. Someone would ask what my mom wanted for her birthday. Three people would answer. Someone would suggest a group gift. Someone else would say "I'll just get her something myself." And then the whole thing would quietly dissolve into four separate gifts, two of which were duplicate candles.

I tried a spreadsheet. It worked for about six months, until my mom borrowed my laptop and saw a tab called "Mom's Birthday — DO NOT SHOW" with her name at the top. She didn't say anything, but she didn't have to. The next time I opened it, she'd added a comment: "I saw this. It's fine. I like candles."

That was the moment I started actually researching wishlist apps. Not casually — I mean properly. I tested about a dozen of them, read the App Store reviews, asked friends what they used, and made a spreadsheet of the spreadsheets. Some were excellent. Some were clearly designed for a different problem. And a few were the closest thing to what I actually needed.

This post is the result of that research. It's a genuine comparison — what each app does well, where it falls short, and how to pick the right one for your situation. I'm obviously partial to the app I helped build, but I'll be honest about that and honest about the others too. If you want the short version: the right app depends on whether you're coordinating gifts for yourself, for a family, or for a large group where surprise matters. And almost all of them get the surprise part wrong.

## What Makes a Wishlist App Good?

Before the list, let me set the criteria. I evaluated each app against six things that actually matter for real-world gift coordination.

**Ease of sharing for the giver.** Can someone view and reserve a gift without creating an account or downloading an app? This matters enormously for extended family, coworkers, and older relatives. Anything that adds friction loses participants.

**Surprise protection.** Can the recipient see reservations? This is the single biggest differentiator. Most wishlist apps treat the list owner as an administrator, which means they see every purchase. That's fine for weddings where the couple wants to track gifts. It's a disaster for birthdays, holidays, and anything where surprise matters.

**Duplicate prevention.** Can two people accidentally claim the same item? A good app makes duplication essentially impossible, which is half the reason to use a wishlist in the first place.

**Group gifting.** Can multiple people chip in on one item? This is important for bigger gifts — a stroller, a piece of furniture, a trip — where no single person wants to spend the full amount.

**List quality.** Is the list easy to build and pleasant to browse? Mobile-first design is non-negotiable in 2027. If the app is ugly on a phone, people won't use it.

**Price.** Free is best, but a reasonable subscription is fine if the app actually delivers. I'll flag the cost for each.

## The Best Wishlist Apps, Compared

Here are the apps I've tested and used, with an honest assessment of each.

### 1. Giftly Lists (iOS) — Best for Surprise Protection and Group Gifting

Full disclosure: I helped build this one. But I built it because I couldn't find an existing app that solved the problem I had — keeping surprises intact while allowing a group to coordinate.

**What it does well:**

- Hidden reservations are the core design. The list owner sees only their items; givers see what's been claimed. This is the one thing almost every other app gets wrong.
- No account required to view or reserve. Givers open a link, see the list, tap to claim. That frictionless access makes it usable for grandparents and non-tech-savvy relatives.
- Group contributions are built in, so multiple people can chip in on a single item.
- It's mobile-first, which sounds obvious but isn't — most wishlist apps are still designed desktop-first and it shows.
- Free tier covers up to 3 lists, which is enough for a lot of people. Pro is $1.99/month for up to 25 lists and no ads.

**Where it's weaker:**

- It's iOS only. If your family is on Android, this is a blocker. That's a real limitation and I won't pretend otherwise.
- There's no browser-based version, so someone on a laptop has to use their phone.
- It's newer than the big names, so it has fewer integrations and no retailer partnerships.

If surprise protection is your priority and you're an iOS household, this is the app to try. If you're not, keep reading — the others have real strengths.

### 2. Amazon Wishlist — Best for Pure Convenience

Amazon's wishlist is the most-used wishlist product in the world, for obvious reasons. If you're already buying things on Amazon, adding them to a list is one tap.

**What it does well:**

- The buying experience is frictionless if the giver is also on Amazon. Two taps and the gift is on its way.
- The catalog is enormous. Anything you can buy on Amazon can go on the list.
- It's free and integrated into an app most people already have.

**Where it falls short:**

- The recipient sees everything. Amazon shows you when an item has been purchased, and there's no way to hide it. For a birthday or holiday, that's a spoiler waiting to happen.
- If you want to coordinate a group gift, the workaround is to add a note to an item, which is clumsy.
- It only works for Amazon products. Anything from a local shop, an independent artist, or a service-based gift can't be on the list.

If you're shopping for a wedding where you want to track what's coming, Amazon's approach is fine. For surprise protection, it's the wrong tool. My post on [how to keep gifts a surprise](/blog/how-to-keep-gifts-a-surprise/) explains why.

### 3. Elfster — Best for Secret Santa and Large Groups

Elfster has been around for over a decade and is probably the best-known dedicated wishlist app. It's built around gift exchanges, which is its strength and its limitation.

**What it does well:**

- Secret Santa is its core feature, and it does it well. Name draws, exclusions for couples, wishlist integration — it's all there.
- Large group exchanges work smoothly. If you're organizing a 30-person office exchange, Elfster handles the logistics.
- The wishlist functionality is decent, with product links and notes.

**Where it falls short:**

- The interface feels dated compared to newer apps.
- Surprise protection is partial — the recipient can't see all reservations, but the app's default is transparent in ways that aren't ideal for pure surprise situations.
- It's really built for exchanges, not for someone maintaining a year-round wishlist.
- Ad-supported on the free tier.

If you're organizing a Secret Santa specifically, Elfster is a strong choice — I wrote a full comparison of the Secret Santa setup in my [Secret Santa guide](/blog/secret-santa-surprise/). For general wishlist coordination, there are better options.

### 4. Giftster — Best for Multi-Family Coordination

Giftster is designed for families who coordinate gifts across many households. It's more traditional in its design but genuinely useful for large family networks.

**What it does well:**

- Family groups are the core unit, which makes it easy to manage multiple lists in one place.
- It handles the "who's buying for whom" complexity of large families well.
- The browser version is solid, which matters if you have relatives who don't use apps.

**Where it falls short:**

- The design feels dated, especially on mobile.
- Surprise protection isn't built in as a default — you have to work around it.
- It's harder to use for one-off occasions like a single birthday. It's built for ongoing family coordination.

If you're managing a big extended family with multiple gift occasions per year and you want a single system, Giftster is a reasonable choice. If you're a smaller group or you care most about surprise, look elsewhere.

### 5. Babylist — Best for Baby Registries Specifically

Babylist solved a specific problem beautifully: letting expectant parents register for items from multiple stores in one place. It's now the dominant baby registry app.

**What it does well:**

- You can add items from any store to one registry, which is genuinely useful.
- The gift-giver experience is clean, and the "group gift" feature works well for bigger purchases.
- It handles the "must-have" checklist side of baby prep, which new parents find useful.

**Where it falls short:**

- It's a baby registry, not a general wishlist app. It's not designed for birthdays or holidays.
- The recipient sees every purchase. That's the standard registry model, and it spoils the surprise at the shower.
- The iOS app is heavier than it needs to be.

If you're having a baby, Babylist is probably the right tool. If you want a surprise shower — and some parents do, as I wrote about in the [baby shower guide](/blog/baby-shower-surprise-gift-list/) — you'll want a parallel hidden-reservation list.

### 6. Zola — Best for Weddings

Zola is the dominant wedding registry platform, and for good reason. It handles the complexities of wedding registries — multiple stores, cash funds, experiences — in one place.

**What it does well:**

- Wedding-specific features like cash funds, honeymoon contributions, and experience gifts are all built in.
- The design is elegant, which matters for the wedding aesthetic.
- Givers can see what's available and purchase directly.

**Where it falls short:**

- It's a wedding platform. It doesn't work for birthdays, holidays, or general gift coordination.
- The recipient sees every purchase, which is the standard registry model. Some couples love that; some want the surprise of the shower, which I covered in the [wedding gift guide](/blog/wedding-gift-surprise/).

If you're registering for a wedding, Zola is a strong choice. It's not a general wishlist app.

### 7. The Notes App (Yes, Really)

I'm including this because it's what a lot of people actually use, and it's worth acknowledging why.

**What it does well:**

- It's already on your phone. Zero friction.
- You can write whatever you want, in whatever format you want.
- It's private by default, which means no accidental sharing.

**Where it falls short:**

- It's not shareable. You have to screenshot it or copy the text, which loses the structure and the ability to update.
- No duplicate prevention. No group coordination.
- No surprise protection because there's no reservation layer at all.

The notes app is a fine place to _start_ a list — I actually recommend keeping a running note throughout the year, as I wrote in my post on [how to make a Christmas list](/blog/how-to-make-a-christmas-list/). But it's not a coordination tool.

## How to Choose the Right App for You

Here's a decision framework. Match the app to the situation, not the other way around.

**If surprise matters most** — birthdays, holidays, showers where the recipient wants to be surprised — choose an app with genuine hidden reservations. Giftly Lists is built for this. The others aren't.

**If you're organizing a Secret Santa** — Elfster is a strong choice, and its exchange features are genuinely good.

**If you're registering for a wedding or a baby** — Zola and Babylist are the right tools. They're designed for those specific registries, and they handle the complexity well.

**If you're managing a large extended family across many occasions** — Giftster is the most natural fit, though the design is dated.

**If you're just buying from Amazon anyway** — Amazon's wishlist is fine for that. It's not a coordination tool, but it doesn't need to be.

**If you're coordinating a group gift** — use an app with contribution features. Giftly Lists and Elfster both do this, though Giftly Lists handles the surprise layer better.

## A Personal Story: The App That Finally Worked for My Family

Let me close the loop on my own story.

After the spreadsheet disaster, I tried a few of these apps with my family. Amazon's wishlist didn't work because half the gifts were from local shops. Elfster didn't work because the interface confused my mom. Giftster was too complicated for a family that just wanted to coordinate one birthday.

What finally worked was a hidden-reservation list. My family could browse without creating accounts. The givers could see what was claimed. My mom could not. The whole thing took about fifteen minutes to set up, and it worked on the first try.

The first birthday we used it for, my mom unwrapped a set of pottery tools she'd mentioned wanting six months earlier, a cookbook from a local chef, and a contribution toward a weekend away with my dad. She was genuinely surprised by all three. My brother, who had never bought a thoughtful gift in his life, told me it was the first time he'd felt confident about what he was giving.

That's the thing a good wishlist app does. It's not about the app. It's about the moment when someone opens a gift and realizes that a group of people coordinated quietly on their behalf, without them ever catching a clue. The app is just the scaffolding.

## The Bottom Line

There's no single best wishlist app. There's a best app for your specific situation, and it depends on what you're coordinating and how much surprise matters.

If you're doing a wedding or a baby registry, use Zola or Babylist. If you're organizing a Secret Santa, use Elfster. If you're managing a big family with many occasions, Giftster is reasonable.

If you want the thing almost every app misses — a wishlist where the givers can coordinate and the recipient stays genuinely surprised — that's what Giftly Lists was built for. It's iOS-only, which is a real limitation, and it's newer than the big names. But the design choice at its core, hidden reservations, is the thing that makes it work.

Whatever you choose, choose it intentionally. A wishlist app is only as good as the coordination it enables. And the coordination is what makes the gift feel like more than the item.

That's the whole thing.
