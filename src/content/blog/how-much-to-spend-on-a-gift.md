The most stressful gift I ever gave cost me eleven dollars. It was a housewarming present for a couple who'd just bought their first place. I'd spent two weeks agonizing over it — not because I couldn't find anything, but because I couldn't figure out how much I was _supposed_ to spend. They were close friends. I'd been to their wedding. I'd helped them move. Eleven dollars felt insulting. Fifty felt like showing off. I landed on a nice olive oil and a good loaf of bread from a local bakery, wrapped it in a tea towel, and handed it over with my heart in my throat. They loved it. They used the tea towel for years.

But I didn't know they would. I spent those two weeks in genuine distress, and the reason is that nobody ever tells you the rules. Gift budgets are one of those things everyone navigates privately and slightly anxiously, and the lack of clear guidance means most of us are guessing. We guess wrong sometimes. We overspend and resent it. We underspend and feel guilty. We compare ourselves to a hypothetical standard that doesn't actually exist.

So let me do what nobody did for me. Let me write down what I've learned after years of coordinating gifts for weddings, baby showers, birthdays, retirements, teachers, coaches, and coworkers — including a few occasions where I badly overspent, and a few where I realized I'd been wildly overthinking it.

## The Truth Nobody Wants to Admit About Gift Budgets

First, the part that's hard to hear: there is no universal rule. Anyone who tells you there is one is either selling something or repeating something they heard from their grandmother.

What actually determines how much you should spend is a combination of four things:

1. **Your relationship to the person.** A coworker you like is not the same as your best friend since college.
2. **Your actual financial situation.** Not your aspirational one. Your actual one.
3. **The occasion.** A wedding is different from a housewarming, which is different from a Tuesday.
4. **Whether you're giving alone or as part of a group.** This is the one most people forget, and it changes everything.

That fourth point is the key. Almost all gift-budget stress comes from forgetting that group gifting exists. You don't have to spend $200 on your sister's wedding gift alone. You can chip in $40 toward something meaningful with five other people and end up giving her something far better than you could have managed solo. I've written about this before in my [group gift coordination guide](/blog/group-gift-coordination-guide/), and it's genuinely the single biggest unlock in this whole conversation. Group gifting lets you give better gifts for less money, which is the closest thing to a free lunch that exists in the gift world.

Now let's get into the specifics.

## Weddings: The Most Anxious Gift of All

Wedding gifts cause more stress than any other category, partly because the numbers involved can be enormous, and partly because there's a persistent myth that you need to "cover your plate."

Let me be direct about this: the plate myth is not a rule. It's a bit of folk wisdom that got passed around so much that people treat it as law. It originated at a time and in a context where it made more sense, and it has never been a universal standard. You are not obligated to spend the cost of your dinner on a gift. You are obligated to be gracious, show up if you said you would, and give what you can reasonably afford.

A realistic breakdown, based on what I've observed and what etiquette experts generally say:

- **Close family and very close friends:** $100 to $200, or a group contribution of any amount toward something significant.
- **Extended family and good friends:** $50 to $100.
- **Coworkers and acquaintances:** $25 to $50, or a group gift.
- **If you're traveling far for the wedding:** your presence genuinely is the gift. A smaller gift is completely appropriate.
- **If you're in the wedding party:** you're already spending on attire, travel, and events. A modest gift is fine.
- **If you can't afford much:** give what you can, or give something handmade. A thoughtful $25 gift beats a resentful $200 gift every time.

The group option is the move here. Six friends chipping in $40 each gives the couple a $240 gift that actually matters — something off their list that they'll use for years. That's far better than six separate $40 gifts they'll have to find space for. I wrote a full piece on [keeping wedding gifts a surprise](/blog/wedding-gift-surprise/) if you're coordinating one and want to avoid spoiling it.

## Baby Showers: Generous but Practical

Baby showers have their own logic, and it's gentler than weddings. The parents are about to spend an absurd amount of money on a human being, so gifts tend to focus on practical help.

- **Close friends and family:** $50 to $100.
- **Extended family and friends:** $25 to $50.
- **Coworkers and casual friends:** $20 to $40.
- **Group gifts:** very common, and very welcome. A group contribution toward a stroller or a car seat is often the most appreciated gift of all.

One thing worth knowing: baby registries usually have a wide range of price points for exactly this reason. You can spend $15 on a pack of swaddles or $300 on a crib. Both are appropriate. The parents will use both. If you're coordinating with others, my [baby shower guide](/blog/baby-shower-surprise-gift-list/) covers how to do it without the shower becoming a logistics nightmare.

## Birthdays: All Over the Place, on Purpose

Birthday budgets vary wildly depending on who it's for and what kind of birthday it is.

- **Partner or spouse:** whatever your shared budget allows. This is a conversation to have, not a guess to make.
- **Kids' birthday parties:** $15 to $30. This is a party gift, not a lifetime commitment. Most parents genuinely do not want you to spend more.
- **Close friends:** $25 to $75.
- **Extended family:** $25 to $50.
- **Coworkers:** $10 to $25, or a group contribution.
- **Milestone birthdays (30th, 40th, 50th):** higher, and often group-based. I wrote about this at length in my [milestone birthday guide](/blog/milestone-birthday-gift-surprise/).

Here's something I've learned the hard way: the price of the gift matters far less than the specificity. A $20 gift that shows you were paying attention beats a $100 gift that shows you weren't. I once gave my dad a $180 tool set he never used and a $12 book about a subject he loved that he still talks about. Guess which one mattered.

## Kids' Birthday Parties: A Special Case

Kids' birthday parties deserve their own section because the anxiety levels are weirdly high.

The standard in most of the US sits between $15 and $30. If your kid is going to a party at a bounce house with 25 other kids, you don't need to buy the birthday kid a $50 Lego set. A $20 gift, thoughtfully chosen, is perfect. Many parents now include a wishlist link on the invitation, which makes this dramatically easier — I've written about that in my [kids' birthday gift guide](/blog/kids-birthday-gift-surprise/).

Two things to know:

- **Sibling parties:** if two kids in the same family are having a combined party, don't feel obligated to double your spend. One $25 gift for the pair is fine.
- **Classmates:** $15 is genuinely plenty. The kid won't remember what you spent. They'll remember what they got, which is why a wishlist helps so much.

## Holidays: The Big One

Holiday gift budgets are the most personal of all, because they usually involve an internal family negotiation rather than a social norm.

A few frameworks that work:

- **Set a per-person cap and stick to it.** Families that do this report far less stress. It also prevents the awkwardness of one person giving a big gift while everyone else gives small ones.
- **Do a Secret Santa or gift exchange.** This is the single best way to keep holiday spending under control in a large group. Everyone buys one gift instead of ten. I wrote about how to [keep a Secret Santa exchange from going sideways](/blog/secret-santa-surprise/) — and about how to make it surprising rather than stressful.
- **Start early.** I've written a whole piece on [why Christmas surprises start in July](/blog/why-christmas-surprises-start-in-july/), and a big part of the argument is financial. Spreading holiday spending over several months is much easier than cramming it into December.
- **Go experience-based.** A group contribution toward a family trip or a nice dinner out is often better than five small gifts nobody remembers.

If you want the formal gift-exchange piece, my [holiday gift exchange guide](/blog/holiday-gift-exchange-surprise/) covers the coordination side in detail.

## Teachers and Coaches: Small, Coordinated, Meaningful

This is a category where group gifting isn't just nice — it's basically the standard.

- **Individual gift from one family:** $15 to $30, or a gift card.
- **Group gift from a class or team:** $5 to $20 per family, pooled. The teacher or coach ends up with something significantly better than they'd get from any single family.
- **End of year, holidays, or a milestone departure:** slightly more, again group-based.

The thing to remember here is that teachers and coaches receive a _lot_ of small generic gifts. A class that pools $10 per family across 20 families can give a $200 gift that's actually meaningful. I wrote detailed guides on both [teacher appreciation gifts](/blog/teacher-appreciation-gift-surprise/) and [coach gifts](/blog/coach-gift-surprise/) if you're coordinating one.

## Graduations, Housewarmings, Retirements: The Mid-Tier

These fall in a middle zone that people find oddly confusing.

- **Graduation (high school):** $25 to $50, or a group gift.
- **Graduation (college):** $50 to $100.
- **Housewarming:** $20 to $50. Or a nice consumable — olive oil, wine, a good loaf of bread — which costs less and lands better than you'd think.
- **Retirement:** $50 to $150 individually, or a group contribution toward something significant. Retirement is a major life transition, so a group gift often fits beautifully. My [retirement gift guide](/blog/retirement-gift-surprise/) goes into this at length.
- **Baby's arrival (not a shower):** $25 to $50, or a meal, or help.

## Coworkers: Err Small

Office gifts are a minefield because you have to navigate both relationship dynamics and the risk of creating awkwardness.

- **Team gift for a departing coworker:** $5 to $20 per person, pooled.
- **Secret Santa:** set a cap. $15 to $25 is standard.
- **Birthday among a close team:** $10 to $25, or a group card.
- **Promotion, new job, new baby:** $10 to $30, or a contribution.

The trick with coworker gifts is to keep them neutral in scale. A gift that's too expensive can create a sense of obligation. A group contribution spread across a team keeps it comfortable for everyone. I wrote about the mechanics of a graceful [farewell gift for a coworker](/blog/farewell-gift-coworker-surprise/) if you're in that situation now.

## The Group Gift Math That Changes Everything

Here's the single most useful thing I've ever learned about gift budgets. It's a small piece of arithmetic, but it shifts the entire conversation.

If you're buying alone, your budget is your budget. If you're buying as a group, your budget is your budget _plus_ everyone else's.

Ten people contributing $20 each gives a $200 gift. Fifteen contributing $25 each gives $375. Twenty contributing $15 each gives $300.

This means you can give something genuinely significant — a real wedding gift, a meaningful retirement present, a trip, a piece of art — without any single person stretching. The result is a gift that feels thoughtful and generous from the recipient's perspective and affordable from the giver's. It's the rare situation where everyone wins.

The only thing that makes this hard is coordination. Which is why the hidden-reservation model exists. I wrote about it in depth in [how to keep gifts a surprise](/blog/how-to-keep-gifts-a-surprise/) and [the art of surprise](/blog/the-art-of-surprise/), but the short version is: everyone can see what's been claimed, and the recipient can't see any of it. No spreadsheets. No chasing. No spoilers.

## What to Do When You Genuinely Can't Afford to Give

This deserves a direct answer, because it comes up more than people admit.

You are allowed to give less than you think you should. You are allowed to give nothing but a card with a handwritten note. You are allowed to skip a wedding you can't afford to attend properly. You are allowed to bring a homemade gift. You are allowed to say, honestly, "I wish I could do more, but I'm in a tight season right now."

People remember generosity of spirit far more than they remember dollar amounts. And almost nobody is actually keeping score — the people who are keeping score are not the people whose opinions should shape your spending.

One practical option: if you're part of a group gift, contribute whatever you can, even if it's $5. Group gifts aren't assigned evenly. The person who puts in $5 and the person who puts in $100 both get to be part of the same meaningful gift, and the recipient never sees the breakdown.

## A Word About Making Your Own List

If you're the one _receiving_ gifts this year, you can make everyone else's budget anxiety much easier by building a clear wishlist with a wide range of price points. I wrote about this at length in [what to put on a wishlist](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-wishlist/) and [how to share a wishlist without feeling greedy](/blog/share-wishlist-without-greed/), but the core idea is simple: give people options at $10, $50, and $150, and let them choose what fits their lives. That's not demanding. That's considerate.

I still think about that eleven-dollar housewarming gift sometimes. The truth is, my friends didn't care what I spent. They cared that I showed up, that I thought about them, and that the olive oil was genuinely good. All the agonizing I did over the number was invisible to them and exhausting to me.

The number matters less than you think. What matters is that you gave something, that it came from a place of care, and that you didn't wreck your own budget in the process. Everything else is noise.
